The Suit That Fits: What Tailoring Actually Costs, and When It Is Worth It

THE SUIT THAT FITS · NOVEMBER 19, 2026 · 7 MIN READ

The mirror in the fitting room is not flattering anyone. A man stands in a suit that costs $400, and the shoulders sit a half-inch past his own, the sleeves cover his shirt cuffs, and the trousers pool at the shoe. The salesman says it can be fixed. The tailor, two doors down, says the same thing and names a price: $150. Across town, another man is on his third fitting for a suit that costs $2,850, cut from a single pattern drawn for his body alone, and he will wait six more weeks to wear it. Both men will walk out looking well dressed. Only one of them will have spent his money where it shows.

The question is not whether tailoring is worth it. The question is which kind of tailoring, at which price, for which man — and where the extra money stops buying anything you can see.

Three ways to buy a suit

There are three tiers, and the industry's vocabulary is precise about what each one means. Off the rack is a finished garment in a standard size, bought and then adjusted. Made to measure starts from an existing pattern and alters it to your measurements. Bespoke starts from nothing: a new pattern drafted for one body, cut by hand, fitted three or four times. The prices follow the labour, and the labour is the point.

Off the rack is the cheapest way in. A solid suit from a retailer like J.Crew, Nordstrom, or Suitsupply runs $300 to $600, and professional alterations add $50 to $150, according to 2026 pricing guides from Paperlust — so most men spend $400 to $750 all in. The suit exists today, in your size, and the tailor's work is the difference between wearing it and wearing it well.

Made to measure is the middle tier, and the widest. Entry-level brands start around $500 for a basic build, and premium made-to-measure in better cloth usually lands between $900 and $1,500, per 2026 market pricing compiled by FashionABC. AlphaSuit, a custom tailor operating in Columbus, Cincinnati, and Orlando, starts core-fabric suits at $795 in Columbus and Cincinnati and $875 in Orlando, with premium fabrics and upgraded construction from $1,250 and $1,375 respectively. In New York, the independent made-to-measure field runs from Alton Lane at $399 to Michael Andrews Bespoke at $1,195 and Cad & The Dandy at $2,600, according to Casual Fitters' 2026 New York price guide. One or two fittings, four to eight weeks, and a suit that was cut for your measurements rather than adjusted to them.

Bespoke is a different purchase. Prices often start near $2,000 in the United States and run past $5,000 for Savile Row-level work, and a single suit can take 60 to 80 hours of hand and machine work, per AlphaSuit's 2026 guide. Kuhlman Custom Tailoring in Seattle, a working tailor's studio since 1999, starts a two-piece bespoke suit in Super 130s Italian wool at $2,850 and a three-piece at $3,850, with three to four fittings over six to eight weeks. In New York, a true bespoke suit often starts around $3,500 and can reach $6,000 or more, and the designer houses — Tom Ford and Kiton — start at $6,000 and climb from there.

What the money actually buys

Three things set the price: cloth, construction, and labour. Cloth is the most visible and the least understood. The mills whose names appear on the bolt — Loro Piana, Zegna, Dormeuil — charge for fibre, weave, and finish, and a Super 130s wool drapes differently from a Super 100s because the yarn is finer. But cloth is also where the markup hides. A $2,000 suit is not made from cloth four times better than a $500 suit's. It is made from cloth perhaps twice as good, cut by hands paid far more, in a room you visited three times.

Construction is the quiet variable. A full-canvas jacket carries a layer of horsehair canvas through the whole front, so the chest moulds to the body over time instead of bubbling or stiffening the way a fused jacket can. It adds roughly $100 to $300 or more to the price, per AlphaSuit, and it is the single construction choice that most affects how a suit looks in its third year. A half-canvas jacket splits the difference. A fused jacket, the standard at the low end, is glued — and glue ages worse than canvas.

Labour is the rest. A made-to-measure suit needs one or two fittings. A bespoke suit needs three or more, and each visit is a tailor's hour. The 60 to 80 hours behind a bespoke suit are not a marketing number; they are the reason it costs what it costs, and the reason it fits the way it fits.

The alterations arithmetic

Here is the calculation that matters for most men. A $400 suit plus $150 of alterations is a $550 suit that fits. An $800 suit off the peg that fits no better is an $800 suit that fits no better. The tailor's invoice is itemised, and the items have standard prices: trouser hemming $20 to $50, taking in the waist $75 to $150, shoulder work $150 or more, according to 2026 alteration pricing. A full overhaul of a suit runs four to eight hours at labour rates of $40 to $120 an hour.

The rule that governs all of it is the shoulder rule. A tailor can take in a waist, shorten a sleeve, and hem a trouser. Rebuilding a shoulder is surgery: expensive, risky, and rarely worth doing. Buy the suit whose shoulders fit, and let the tailor fix everything below them. That one rule, applied in the shop, saves more money than any sale.

Buy the suit whose shoulders fit. Everything below them is the tailor's job — and the tailor's job is cheap.

The counterargument: the suit you never wear

None of this holds if the suit stays in the closet. The most expensive suit a man owns is usually the one he bought for an occasion that never repeated — the wedding suit, the interview suit, the suit bought on sale in a colour he would never choose at full price. Cost per wear is the only honest denominator, and a $2,850 bespoke suit worn twice a week for five years costs less per wearing than a $400 suit worn twice and abandoned.

Tailoring has limits, too. It cannot fix a shoulder that does not fit, a pattern that fights the body, or a cloth that looks cheap under office light. And there are men for whom the right answer is not a better suit but fewer suits: one navy, one charcoal, both fitted properly, rotated with different shirts and ties. The wardrobe that wins is rarely the biggest one.

The implication is a spending order, not a spending level. First, the fit — and fit is bought from the tailor, not the label, at $50 to $150 a suit. Second, the construction — full canvas if the suit will be worn weekly. Third, the cloth — finer wool once the first two are settled. The $500 to $800 zone is where most men get the most for their money: a good off-the-rack suit, or an entry made-to-measure, with proper alterations. Above $1,500, you are buying the tailor's time and the cloth's name, and both are worth it only if you will wear the result often enough to notice. The mirror knows the difference. So does the calendar.
