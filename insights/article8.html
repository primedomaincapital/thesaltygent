The Etiquette of Dressing Down: How to Wear Casual Without Looking Careless

DRESSING DOWN · NOVEMBER 19, 2026 · 7 MIN READ

Friday morning, the office dress code loosens, and a man who looked sharp on Thursday walks in looking like he slept in his clothes. Nothing he is wearing is wrong, exactly. The polo is clean. The chinos fit. The sneakers are new. But the whole of it adds up to less than the sum of its parts, and everyone in the room can feel it without being able to name it. That is the problem with dressing down: there is no tie to blame, no jacket to hide behind, and no rulebook on the wall. The standards are still there. They are just quieter.

Casual is not the absence of standards. It is a different set of them, and the difference between relaxed and careless is about fifteen minutes of attention.

Fit first, fabric second, logo never

The hierarchy has not changed since the suit went into the closet. Fit beats everything. The style guides are blunt about it: a $200 jacket tailored properly looks richer than a $2,000 jacket off the rack, as Real Men Real Style puts it in its 2026 quiet-luxury guide. The same is true of a $40 polo. A shirt that pulls at the chest or balloons at the waist announces itself before anything else about the outfit registers, and no label fixes it.

Fabric is second, and it is where casual dressing is won or lost. The quiet-luxury canon is specific: cashmere for sweaters and scarves, fine merino wool — Super 120s or higher — for knitwear, Italian flannel for cold-weather trousers, full-grain leather for shoes and belts, Egyptian or Supima cotton in 2-ply construction for shirts. The warning is equally specific: not "genuine leather," which is the worst grade of leather despite the reassuring name, and not polyester blends in anything meant to look like a serious garment. Cloth has depth and drape you can see from across a room, and synthetic cloth has neither.

Logo never. The single clearest marker of the look that replaced loud branding is the absence of branding. Quiet luxury — the phrase became widely used in 2023, when coverage of the costumes in the HBO series Succession spread the idea, as CNBC reported in June of that year — is built on the assumption that anyone worth impressing already knows what good cloth looks like. Logan Roy's baseball cap was the point: the cap was ordinary, and the man wearing it was not.

The palette is the whole game

If there is one rule to take from the quiet-luxury guides, it is the palette. Navy, charcoal, camel, cream, stone, olive, chocolate brown, oatmeal, soft grey. That is the entire menu, and the discipline is in staying inside it. The trick is tonal dressing: layering shades that live near each other — a camel coat over a cream sweater over oatmeal trousers, a navy blazer with charcoal trousers and a grey crewneck underneath — instead of contrasting colours that compete for attention.

The silhouette has not changed either. The old-money line is fitted without restriction: not slim-fit in the contemporary sense, and not relaxed or oversized. A well-cut wool trouser in a fine worsted weave speaks for itself — the cloth has a quiet sheen, the break at the ankle is correct, the waistband sits without pulling. A man in a perfectly fitted oatmeal polo and pressed trousers reads as more polished than a man in a logo-covered outfit three times the price.

Three uniforms that work

The first is the knit polo and trousers: a long-sleeve knit polo in navy or cream, pressed wool trousers, loafers. It works at lunch, at the club, and at a dinner where a jacket would be too much. The second is the unstructured blazer and tee: a soft-shouldered blazer in navy or charcoal, a plain white or grey crew-neck tee in heavy cotton, dark denim or chinos, clean leather shoes. The blazer does the formal work; the tee does the relaxing. The third is the chore coat and denim: a canvas or wool chore coat, a henley or flannel shirt, dark straight-leg denim, boots. It is the weekend uniform, and it is the one most men get wrong by letting everything go baggy at once. One relaxed piece per outfit. The rest stays fitted.

The build does not require a fortune. The guides are explicit that the look can be assembled at many prices, from Uniqlo and Sunspel at the accessible end to Loro Piana and Extreme Cashmere at the top, and that a high price or a famous name does not by itself guarantee quality. The Stealth Wealth guide makes the same point with arithmetic: a Tom Ford cashmere sweater that lasts decades against five fast-fashion sweaters that pill after a season. The cheap one is the one you replace.

The shoes decide

The shoes are the part of a casual outfit that the wearer forgets and the room remembers. Clean, well-kept leather shoes — loafers, suede chukkas, a plain derby — rescue an outfit that sneakers would sink, and no amount of good knitwear fixes scuffed trainers at a dinner table. The rule is simple: if the shoes look like they were chosen this morning, the outfit looks chosen. If they look like they were grabbed from the floor, so does everything else. Socks follow the same logic. Dark socks with dark trousers, no-show socks with loafers in summer, and never white athletic crew socks with anything that is not an athletic shoe.

One relaxed piece per outfit. The rest stays fitted. That is the whole secret, and it is the part everyone skips.

The counterargument: casual can read as not caring

Here is the uncomfortable part. Dressing down is judged by the room, not by the wearer, and the same outfit reads differently at a beach bar and at a wedding. The man who dresses casually at a formal occasion is not relaxed; he is telling the room the occasion did not matter to him. Context is the rule that overrides all the others, and it is the one the guides rarely state plainly enough.

There is also the costume risk. Quiet luxury, worn self-consciously, becomes its own kind of showing off — the $900 cashmere crewneck worn to signal that you do not signal. The old-money aesthetic is a specific visual language built on fabric quality, proportion, and restraint, and getting it wrong is just as loud as wearing a head-to-toe monogram. The difference between understated and underdressed is often a single choice: the shoes. Clean, well-kept leather shoes rescue an outfit that sneakers would sink, and no amount of good knitwear fixes scuffed trainers at a dinner table.

The implication is a short checklist, applied before leaving the house. Does it fit — really fit, in the shoulders and the waist? Is the cloth real — wool, cotton, cashmere, leather, not a blend pretending to be one of them? Is there a logo anywhere, and if so, can it be removed? Are the shoes clean? And does the room I am walking into expect this, or am I dressing for the room I wish I were walking into? Answer those five, and casual stops being a risk. It becomes what it was supposed to be: a man who looks like he chose his clothes, rather than a man whose clothes happened to him.
